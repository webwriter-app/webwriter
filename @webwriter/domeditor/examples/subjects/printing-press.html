Effects of technical progress: developments compared

There have been major technical developments at several points in history. Here we look at two of them: the printing press and industrialisation. They are roughly 300 years apart.

Tasks

Compare the effects in the two eras in the context of their respective developments. What can be generalised?

Today, too, many people believe that AI has brought us to a comparable point in time.
Discuss which aspects can be derived from this and which predictions you might be able to make.

Historical overview

Timeline 1: The printing press

A new printing technique

Around 1450 in Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg develops a printing process using movable metal type. The individual letters can be put together to form texts, inked and printed on paper. Afterwards, the same type can be used again for other texts.

What changes? The technique makes it possible to produce large numbers of almost identical copies. A page of the Gutenberg Bible, printed around 1455, shows the result.

Printing workshops and new trades

Printing workshops open in more and more European towns and cities. Typesetters, printers, bookbinders and booksellers, among others, work together to produce and distribute printed material.

What changes? A new trade emerges. The illustration by Jost Amman shows a printing workshop in 1568, so it dates from some time after this development began.

Printed matter for everyday life

Alongside books, smaller printed items appear more and more often: calendars, public notices, broadsheets and other single sheets. Some give information about dates or events, others are meant to entertain.

What changes? Printed information is used for different purposes. The New Moon Calendar of 1482 is an example of such a single-sheet print.

Knowledge about the world

Schedel’s World Chronicle, known in English as the Nuremberg Chronicle, is published in Latin and German in 1493. It combines accounts of history with many woodcuts, including views of cities.

What changes? Printing means that similar texts and images can be distributed in large numbers of copies. However, the illustrations reflect the ideas of their time and are not always geographically accurate.

Science in printed images

In 1543, Andreas Vesalius publishes his work on the human body. The printed edition contains many detailed anatomical illustrations that make medical observations easy to see.

What changes? Printed specialist books make it easier for scholars in different places to refer to the same texts and images. However, this does not mean that everything in them is automatically correct.

Regularly printed news

From 1605, Johann Carolus publishes the newspaper Relation in Strasbourg. It appears regularly and reports on a range of events. Some of the news comes from existing networks of correspondents.

What changes? This creates a new model for distributing news to readers in regular issues. The title page shown here dates from 1609.

Timeline 2: Industrialisation

Machines change textile work

From the second half of the 18th century, new machines become established in textile production in Great Britain. The spinning jenny, for example, makes it possible to spin several threads at the same time.

What changes? With the machine, one worker can produce more yarn than with a simple spinning wheel. The copper engraving from 1811 is a later depiction of a spinning jenny.

Working in factories

More and more machines are brought together in larger works. In many textile mills, the machinery is driven by water power or steam power. Workers operate and supervise the machines.

What changes? Production and everyday working life change: where people work, their working hours and their tasks are increasingly determined by the factory. The illustration dates from 1835.

The railway connects cities

On 7 December 1835, the first German railway runs on the line between Nuremberg and Fürth. Over the following decades, the railway network grows considerably.

What changes? People and goods can be transported more quickly over longer distances. Businesses gain new markets, and travel becomes possible for more groups in society.

Social conflict: the Silesian weavers

Many Silesian weavers earn their living by working at home and fall into economic hardship. In 1844, weavers protest against their situation; it turns into an uprising, which the army puts down.

What changes? The example shows that technical and economic change can have very different consequences for different groups. The political cartoon from 1848 comments on the hardship and is not a photograph of the uprising.

Industrial centres grow

In the 19th century, factories, mining and railways expand in many regions. Industrial centres such as Essen grow; more people look for work there and move close to the works.

What changes? This changes what towns look like and how people live day to day. At the same time, new problems arise, for example with housing, health and basic supplies. The illustration shows a historical view of the Krupp factory.

The state responds with social legislation

In 1883, the Health Insurance Act for workers comes into force in the German Empire. It requires certain groups of employees to have statutory health insurance.

What changes? With this law, the state responds to the social risks of industrial society. However, not everyone was insured at first. The illustration shows the first page of the text of the law.
