Same fence, different garden

You have 24 metres of fencing for a rectangular school garden. Can two gardens use the same fence but leave different amounts of growing space? Compare designs, then test them by dragging a slider.

Compare two plans

Both plans use 24 metres of fencing. Count the sides all the way around: perimeter measures the boundary, while area measures the space inside. The drawings use the same scale.

Change a length

Drag the slider a in the construction below. One side is a metres long and the other is 12 − a metres. Every rectangle still uses 24 metres of fencing, because 2 × (a + 12 − a) = 24. The number inside the green rectangle is its area in square metres.

Try a = 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10. Record the two side lengths and area for each design. Predict the area before moving the slider. Which pairs of designs give the same area?

Explain the pattern

A 6-by-6 square encloses 36 square metres, more than the 32 square metres of a 4-by-8 rectangle. Among rectangles with this fixed perimeter, the square has the greatest area. Turning a rectangle around does not change its area: compare a = 4 and a = 8.

Explain why making one side longer can eventually make the growing space smaller. For an extra challenge, write the area as a × (12 − a) and use your recorded values to check the rule.
