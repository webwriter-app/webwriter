In many places, the Ahr used to have a wider, branching course with several channels, gravel banks and islands. Its course could shift as a result of floods and the deposition of sediment.

The construction of roads and railway lines changed the Ahr valley. To create transport routes and to protect against water, riverbanks were reinforced and sections of the river were altered in various places.

As the villages and towns grew, more and more land along the Ahr was taken up by buildings, roads and other uses. This also changed the space into which the river could spread during floods.

The Ahr over time

Compare the shape of the river. Suggest possible reasons for the changes.

The Ahr has not always looked the way it does today. As late as the 19th century it had a wide course with several channels, gravel banks and islands, especially around what is now Bad Neuenahr.

From 1852 onwards, people began to deliberately straighten and regulate larger sections of the Ahr. The aim was to limit flooding, control the flow of water better and gain additional land for farming. At the same time, areas of standing water were to be removed, as they were regarded as a health problem at the time.

In the following decades, further sections of the river were altered. The construction of roads, railway lines and settlements also meant that the Ahr was left with less space in many places.

So people changed the Ahr over many decades. But what benefits did they hope to gain? And how does changing the course of a river affect the water and the surrounding landscape?

Five years after the flood: what has changed?

Since 2021, new flood protection plans have been developed in the Ahr valley and the first measures have been carried out. In some places, sections of the river have been widened, banks have been reshaped and bridges have been rebuilt with more room for water to flow through.

However, flood protection is still far from complete. Many measures are still being planned or carried out. Funding for larger retention basins has not been secured yet either.

According to the responsible experts, the individual measures carried out so far would not be able to prevent a disaster like the one in 2021.

After the flood disaster, the town of Bad Neuenahr-Ahrweiler faces the task of restoring damaged homes, roads, bridges, schools and public buildings. At the same time, it has to make sure that its people are better protected from flooding in future. This creates a conflict over space: many buildings and important facilities lie very close to the Ahr. If all the areas at risk were given up, a considerable part of the land used for housing so far would be lost.

In early 2022, the town therefore took the position that building close to the river must remain possible in future, provided that buildings are designed with the existing flood risks in mind. In the town’s view, liveable town centres, enough housing and a working tourist infrastructure are essential for the long-term development of the Ahr valley.

One concrete example is the planned Sankt Pius residential park in Bad Neuenahr. On 8 July 2021, just a few days before the disaster, the town council had approved a development plan for eleven blocks of flats with a total of 130 rented flats. During the flood the site was inundated, and it was later included in the newly mapped flood zone. This meant that, among other things, the effects of the planned buildings on the flow of water and possible ways of making up for the lost retention space had to be assessed. In early 2022, it was still unclear whether the project would be approved.

The town is also counting on protective measures that reach beyond its own boundaries. Large retention basins on the upper Ahr could hold back water before it reaches the more densely built-up parts of the town.

By 2026, the town had also drawn up plans for wider sections of the river, flood-adapted bridges and additional retention areas (land that can hold floodwater). At the same time, new parks, places to spend time and leisure facilities are to be created along the Ahr. In this way, the town links flood protection with quality of life and economic recovery. It is calling for faster approvals and quick funding for flood protection beyond the town’s own area.

Key interest:
A town that stays habitable and economically viable in the long term and reduces its flood risks through adapted building and additional protective measures.

For the residents of the Ahr valley, the decision about how to deal with the river in future is directly linked to their own lives. Many people lost their homes, much of what they owned or members of their family in the disaster. Others were able to keep their houses but had to repair extensive damage. For them, rebuilding means not only restoring buildings but also returning to familiar surroundings.

Several reports from 2021 and 2022 show how differently the people affected deal with the flood risk.

The photographer Hans-Jürgen Vollrath lived with his family in a small estate of wooden houses on Eichenweg in Bad Neuenahr, right next to the Ahr. Before the disaster, the residents particularly valued the riverside location, the green shared spaces and the neighbourhood. The flood badly damaged the estate. Afterwards, Vollrath’s wife did not want to return to the house by the river. The example shows how a place that used to be attractive to live in can be seen as a permanent danger after a disaster.

Other people affected, on the other hand, do not want to give up the places where they live. Their families, jobs and friends are there, and often a large part of their wealth as well. Moving is also not always affordable. For owners whose houses are damaged but still usable in particular, rebuilding in the same place can be the most obvious practical solution.

New building projects are also judged differently. In early 2022, the residents Anne Behrens and Willi Smits, who were also affected by the flood, raised concerns about building on more land right next to the Ahr. In particular, they pointed to the loss of former green spaces and asked whether additional buildings should take up even more of the already narrow valley.

These examples show that there is no single position shared by everyone affected by the flood. Some prefer rebuilding with better protective measures, while others would like to live further away from the Ahr or to move away for good.

Key interest:
Personal safety, financial security and the freedom to decide for themselves where they will live in future.

The Struktur- und Genehmigungsdirektion Nord (SGD Nord), the regional planning and approvals authority, acts as the upper water authority and is responsible for important decisions on flood protection and on the use of the flood zones along the Ahr. Its task is to oversee the rebuilding so that people are better protected in future and new construction does not create additional flood risks.

After the flood in July 2021, the riverbed had changed dramatically in many places. Banks had been washed away, new gravel areas had formed and in some places the river had taken a different route. During the clean-up that followed, landowners and building firms tried to restore damaged land. In some places, earth and rubble were piled up, banks were reshaped and parts of the river were narrowed again.

In February 2022, SGD Nord confirmed that some of this work had been carried out without the necessary permits. The authority warned that filling in land can reduce the cross-section available for the water to flow through and destroy natural retention areas. In another flood, this could make the water level rise higher locally or push the water onto other land.

SGD Nord therefore requires any work on the Ahr and its tributaries to be agreed with the responsible water authorities. This applies to public and private land alike. Rebuilding houses in flood zones is also subject to special legal requirements.

The authority distinguishes between different situations: under certain conditions, a damaged building may be repaired. A completely new building or the rebuilding of a destroyed one, however, is assessed differently. In areas at particularly high risk, exceptions are severely restricted.

SGD Nord wants to prevent short-term decisions during the rebuilding from increasing the flood risk in the long term. At the same time, it has to take into account the interests of owners who want to use their land and buildings again. This creates a conflict between rebuilding quickly and assessing the flood risk carefully.

Key interest:
 Legally sound rebuilding that does not put people, buildings or neighbouring properties at additional risk.

The Ahrweiler District Administration sees flood protection as a task that goes beyond the boundaries of individual towns and municipalities. The 2021 flood disaster showed that the amount of water at any one place depends largely on how much water flows in from the whole catchment area upstream. Protective measures in a single municipality can therefore only solve part of the problem.

In September 2022, the district and several municipalities began to develop a joint plan of measures across local boundaries. Later, further towns and districts were brought in so that the entire catchment area of the Ahr, from its source to its mouth, could be taken into account.

The plan follows three approaches. Firstly, large flood retention basins are to store water temporarily during major floods and release it in a controlled way. Secondly, smaller local measures are to improve the flow of water and protect individual settlements. Thirdly, more rainwater is to be held back in forests, on farmland and in floodplains.

Studies by engineering firms identified 17 possible sites for larger retention basins in the Ahr catchment area and one more site on the Vinxtbach stream. The plan also includes 216 supporting measures. The basins are meant to reduce the flow of water towards the places further downstream, especially during large and extreme floods.

The studies also conclude that natural water retention can be effective, especially in small and medium-sized floods. When the amounts of water are exceptionally large, however, its protective effect beyond the local area is considered limited. Such near-natural measures are still included in the plan because they can also reduce erosion, improve habitats and keep water in the landscape during dry periods.

Putting the plan into practice is a big undertaking. The large retention basins are estimated to cost around 1.7 billion euros in total. Before they can be built, the sites must be examined more closely, the effects on nature and the landscape must be assessed, land must be made available and permits must be granted. The funding must also be secured.

The district administration therefore relies on a combination of different measures. In its view, it is not enough just to widen individual sections of the river, nor can comprehensive flood protection be achieved with large retention basins alone.

Key interest:
To reduce the risk of extreme floods through coordinated measures across the entire catchment area, and so protect several municipalities at once.

The Bund für Umwelt und Naturschutz Deutschland (BUND, Friends of the Earth Germany) calls for flood protection that makes more use of the natural properties of rivers, soils and landscapes. In the view of this environmental organisation, rainwater should be held back as close as possible to where it falls. This is meant to stop large amounts of water from reaching streams and rivers within a short time.

In BUND’s opinion, several things need to change for this to happen. Rivers and streams should be given wider corridors in which they can spread out during floods. Former floodplains should be reconnected to the rivers. Fewer sealed surfaces and more permeable soils should help water soak into the ground. Farming and forestry should also be managed so that soils store water for longer and less water runs off the surface quickly.

The organisation also criticises the fact that, in the past, many rivers lost a large part of their natural flood areas to bank reinforcement, dykes, settlements and transport routes. If such areas are won back, they can take in water during floods and slow down the flow locally. At the same time, they provide habitats for plants and animals.

For the Ahr valley, BUND welcomes the approach of including the entire catchment area in flood planning. However, it is critical of the strong focus on 17 large retention basins. These would be very expensive and could have a major impact on nature and the landscape. BUND therefore questions whether the benefits of all the planned basins justify their costs and their impact on the environment.

Instead, the organisation calls for many more smaller measures spread across the entire catchment area. It does not reject technical flood defences in principle, but in BUND’s view they should be used as an addition rather than forming the main part of flood prevention.

The organisation names the planned extension of the A1 motorway through the Ahr catchment area as a further conflict. Additional sealed surfaces can increase surface runoff during heavy rain. BUND sees this as contradicting the aim of holding more water back in the landscape.

On the other hand, the organisation supports the planned widening of sections of the Ahr, for example around Sinzig. Such projects should be given enough weight when roads, bridges and other infrastructure are planned.

Key interest:
Flood prevention through natural water retention, restoring floodplains and reducing the amount of sealed ground – combined with nature conservation and climate protection.

The biologist Wolfgang Büchs has carried out scientific research on the plants, animals and landscape of the Ahr valley. He also looks at the 2021 flood disaster against the background of the long-term changes to the space around the river.

Originally, the Ahr had a branching course with several channels, gravel banks and flood areas, especially in the wider parts of the valley. During the 19th and 20th centuries, the river was regulated in many places. At the same time, roads, railway lines, housing estates and business parks were built on land in the river lowlands that had not been built on before.

As a result, the space into which the Ahr could spread during floods shrank in many places. According to Büchs, this is particularly problematic in densely built-up areas such as Bad Neuenahr. There, many buildings and transport routes are right next to the river, so there is little scope for creating additional flood areas.

Büchs calls for the remaining open spaces near the Ahr to be kept as green spaces and retention areas wherever possible. One example is the land used for the “Pius Gärten” housing project in Bad Neuenahr. Before it was built on, there were trees and shrubs and open areas there, among other things. Büchs believes it is important to protect such areas because they can give the river extra room to spread out during floods.

Another point of criticism concerns the work done immediately after the disaster. The flood had reshaped the riverbed in many places, deposited sediment and in some cases created new channels. In early 2022, nature conservation organisations and Büchs reported that in some places these structures had been filled in again and banks reinforced once more.

Büchs warned that this would bring back earlier narrowings of the river. Instead, the natural flow paths revealed by the flood should be taken into account when planning the rebuilding. He sees chances to give the Ahr more space again, above all between the villages and towns.

His criticism also concerns the long-term costs of the rebuilding: if banks are first built up and reinforced even though they will later have to be widened again for flood protection, this may create extra work that could have been avoided.

Key interest: To protect existing and former flood areas, reduce the places where the river is narrowed and take the natural development of the Ahr into account when planning the rebuilding.
