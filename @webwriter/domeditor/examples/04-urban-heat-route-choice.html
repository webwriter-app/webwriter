Heat, Shade, and the School Route

Reasoning toolkit

Physical process

Shading can reduce direct solar heating of pavement. Surface temperature and air temperature are different measurements.

Look for what this dataset actually measured before making a claim about comfort.

Distance, accessible crossings, bridge width, and maintenance can change a route decision.

Investigate the scenario

Riverton is a fictional town. The council measured four possible walking corridors to school at 14:00 on the same summer day. “Surface temperature” describes the pavement; “shade” records the proportion of the route shaded by trees or buildings. These measurements are fictional and simplified.

Fictional Riverton route data
Route Distance (km) Surface temp (°C) Shade (%) Human features
A Market Street 1.2 39 10 Wide road; two crossings
B Canal Path 1.7 31 65 One narrow bridge
C Park Edge 1.5 33 50 Bench and staffed gate
D Station Road 1.3 37 20 Heavy traffic; signal crossing

Worked interpretation: Route B is coolest and most shaded but is 0.5 km longer than A and has a narrow bridge. Route C is 0.3 km longer than A, 6°C cooler, and includes a bench and staffed gate. Physical geography helps explain heat: dark pavement absorbs solar energy, while shade reduces direct heating. Human geography shapes exposure through road design, traffic, crossings, access, and maintenance.

  1. Read the evidence: Rank routes by surface temperature and separately by distance. Calculate the temperature difference between A and C, then explain why temperature alone cannot decide accessibility.
  2. Make a recommendation: For a class walking at 14:00, choose a route and cite three data points, including one physical and one human feature. State a condition that would make you change your choice.
  3. Plan an intervention: Propose one council change—trees, reflective paving, a crossing, water access, or traffic control. Predict which measure it would affect and how you would evaluate it.
