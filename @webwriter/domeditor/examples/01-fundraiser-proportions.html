Purchasing model

A recipe can scale exactly; a shopping list must use whole packs.

Compare buying 7 × 500 g with 5 × 750 g. Which costs less? Which leaves more filling?

Investigate the scenario

The fictional Year 8 council is planning a fundraiser for 90 visitors. A caterer’s tested recipe serves 12 people and uses 3 loaves, 450 g of filling, and 2.4 L of soup. The shop sells loaves for €1.80, filling in 500 g tubs for €4.25, and soup in 1 L cartons for €2.60. Treat these prices and quantities as a fictional dataset.

Recipe and price data
Item For 12 For 90 Pack price
Loaves 3 22.5 €1.80 each
Filling 450 g 3,375 g €4.25 per 500 g
Soup 2.4 L 18 L €2.60 per litre

Worked reasoning: The scale factor is 90 ÷ 12 = 7.5. Filling is 450 × 7.5 = 3,375 g. Because packs cannot be bought as fractions, 3,375 ÷ 500 = 6.75, so buy 7 tubs: 7 × €4.25 = €29.75. Buy 23 loaves, since 22.5 must round up. Soup costs 18 × €2.60 = €46.80. The total is (23 × €1.80) + €29.75 + €46.80 = €117.95. The rounded purchasing step means the shopping list is not exactly proportional, even though the recipe is.

  1. Audit the model: Recalculate each 90-person quantity and identify where rounding is required. State whether rounding up protects against shortage.
  2. Compare unit rates: A second fictional supplier sells 750 g of filling for €5.70. Calculate €/100 g for each supplier and recommend one, explaining the trade-off between price and packaging.
  3. Make a decision: The council has a €130 budget and expects €2.50 per serving. Using the original supplier first, calculate the planned surplus before other costs, then write one assumption that could change the result.
