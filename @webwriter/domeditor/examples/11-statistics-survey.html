Can this survey speak for everyone?

Your mission

A school committee asks whether students want a later library closing time. A volunteer surveys friends already using the library. Evaluate the evidence before writing a recommendation. All responses and study times below are fictional classroom data.

1. Separate the questions

The volunteer asks twelve library users how many hours they studied independently last week and whether they want later opening. Ten say yes. The committee wants to know what all students want, including those who rarely use the library.

Fictional independent-study hours for twelve respondents
Respondents Hours
1–6 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4
7–12 5, 5, 6, 6, 7, 23

Predict whether the mean is above or below the median. Explain which single value has the greatest effect on the mean.

2. Calculate, then question

Run the code. Change the last value from 23 to 8 as a hypothetical correction and compare the two summaries. Do not discard a value merely because it is unusual: a real correction would require checking the original response.

“Ten out of twelve said yes, so around 83% of all students support later opening.”

The volunteer’s draft conclusion

Distinguish a correct description of these twelve respondents from a justified inference about the whole school. Changing the measure of centre does not fix how people were selected.

3. Design a stronger study

Which is the clearest limitation of the claim about all students?

 Twelve is an even number. The sample includes only the volunteer’s friends using the library. The median is lower than the mean.

Removing the value 23 would make this convenience sample representative of the school.

Peer review: Check whether the plan includes different year groups and students who do not already use the library.
